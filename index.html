Three of your forty evenings are being graded.
Nobody will tell you which.

A text management game about running a fine-dining kitchen. Six cooks, four stations, six courses a night — and a critic who never books under his own name.

Open the kitchen

One season is about forty minutes. Nothing to install.

The bar moves when you succeed

Every plate is judged against one number, and that number climbs with your reputation. Cook well and the standard rises to meet you. There is no difficulty setting, because the difficulty is the score.

The suspicion figure is real

That percentage in the corner is a Bayesian posterior over nine tells the room gives off. It is calibrated to within one point, measured across a hundred thousand evenings. When it says 62 %, the guide really is out there 62 times in a hundred.

Every trade is priced before you take it

Lean on a station and the game shows you the odds first — star plate 4 % → 14 %, defect 13 % → 26 % — and then charges you the wear. Nothing is adjusted behind your back and nothing is rounded in your favour.

What it is not

  • There are no graphics. It is text, numbers, and one brass colour.
  • Nothing is real-time. You decide, then you watch the evening play back.
  • It is finished enough to play end to end, and rough in places I already know about.
  • Made by one person. Czech and English, switchable mid-game.

I am after playtesters, not applause

Play until you get bored and then stop — where you stopped is the single most useful thing you can tell me, and it is worth more than a compliment.

Three questions, if you have a minute afterwards:

  1. Before you pressed Start service the first time — what did you think was going to go wrong that evening?
  2. Which evening number were you on when you stopped, and what were you doing?
  3. Paste your kitchen code. It is on the end-of-season screen, and it lets me replay the exact season you played.
